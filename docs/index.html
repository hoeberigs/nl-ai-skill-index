01

The tools employers name, and how they moved

Every advert is read for the technologies it names. Rank each skill by its share of all mentions in a month, and the movement between April and August is the index.

Ranks rather than raw shares, because adverts have grown steadily vaguer over the period and that drags every share down at once. Rank asks the more useful question: of the tools employers still bother to name, which ones?

02

Adverts are naming fewer tools every month

This is the single most consistent movement in the data, and it is why the index is built on rank. Read as raw percentages, almost every technology in the market appears to be declining at once, which says more about how adverts are written than about what employers need.

03

What actually moved

Each panel is one technology's share of mentions, month by month, with a 95% interval. Only the coloured panels cleared significance. The rest are shown at the same scale so the reader can see how little most of them moved.

04

Which roles employers cannot fill

Because the same boards were captured every day for six months, each advert can be followed from the day it appears to the day it goes. Roles whose adverts run longest are the ones the market is struggling to close.

Roles here are discovered from the adverts themselves rather than taken from a fixed list: every vacancy is embedded with a multilingual model and clustered, so the groupings reflect how Dutch employers actually write about work.

05

Which technologies travel together

Lift, not raw counts. Raw co-occurrence just re-ranks the most common skills against one another; lift asks how much more often two technologies appear together than they would if employers picked them independently.

06

Who is hiring, and who keeps re-posting

Advert count alone flatters aggregators and agencies. Median days advertised and repost count together separate genuine hiring from a permanent shop window.

07

How this was measured, and where it can mislead

Two corrections do most of the work here. Both are the difference between a number that looks reasonable and one that is right.

The full method

Pipeline

  1. An upstream tracker scrapes Dutch job boards daily and commits its database, giving this analysis a versioned, reproducible input.
  2. Rows are one per advert per scrape. They collapse to a vacancy identity on normalised title and employer, merging recruiter repost chains that would otherwise fragment one role into many short spells.
  3. Detection probability and its day-to-day persistence are measured on days a vacancy was provably live. The grace period for declaring an advert gone is sized from the persistence parameter.
  4. Survival is Kaplan-Meier with log-rank tests. Left-censored spells are dropped, right-censored spells retained.
  5. Vacancies are embedded with a multilingual MiniLM encoder, reduced by PCA and clustered with k-means, k chosen by silhouette.
  6. Skill trends use a Cochran-Armitage test for trend across months, with Wilson intervals on each point.

Where it can mislead

  • An advert disappearing is not a hire. Roles leave a board when filled, withdrawn, expired or defunded, and this data cannot separate those. Everything here measures time advertised, never time to hire.
  • Skills are read from advert text, and text was retrieved for of vacancies. Every skill figure uses that subset as its denominator, never the full panel.
  • Two boards are not the market. Roles filled through networks, agencies or internal moves never appear.
  • These were removed from the demand index by an explicit rule, and kept in the survival analysis where the contrast is informative.
  • Clusters summarise a continuum. Silhouette scores near 0.15 mean adjacent roles genuinely shade into one another rather than forming discrete types.
